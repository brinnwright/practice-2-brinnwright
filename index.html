The Bauhaus

A German art school that ran for fourteen years, was shut down by politics, and still decides what most of the things around you look like.

Art into Industry   The 1923 slogan

One school, three cities, fourteen years

The Bauhaus was founded in 1919 in Weimar by the German architect Walter Gropius. Its aim, in the Met's words, was a radical one: to reimagine the material world to reflect the unity of all the arts. Gropius set it out in the Proclamation of the Bauhaus that same year, describing a craft guild that would fold architecture, sculpture, and painting back into a single thing.

Every student started in the same place. The preliminary course put them through materials, color theory and formal relationships before they were allowed to specialise, and it was taught by painters, Paul Klee and Vasily Kandinsky and Josef Albers among them. Students arrived from wildly different backgrounds, and the course was the leveller.

The original craft-first plan turned out to be financially impossible. In 1923 Gropius repositioned the shcool around designing for mass production, and it took a new slogan: Art into Industry. In 1925 the whole school moved to Dessau, into a building Gropius designed for it.
